Thesis of the week: In September, fewer Monroe County homes sold than a year earlier (216 vs. 251, −13.9%). Redfin's price measure barely moved (−1.9%). Fewer sales is not the same thing as lower values, and the numbers circulating online are being misread in both directions.

Slate: 26 original assignments (reduced from 30 for Thanksgiving week and thin research, as approved), plus 3 Pocono Ledger posts, 1 Nextdoor post and 1 YouTube long-form. Every reel runs 30 seconds or less. Instagram and TikTok each carry 2 shock pieces.

Are Monroe County Home Prices Falling? What the Latest Data Actually Shows

Short answer: Not clearly. The latest verified Monroe County data, for September 2026, shows fewer homes selling: 216 sales versus 251 a year earlier, a 13.9% drop, according to Redfin. Redfin's median sale price measure was down only about 2%. Fewer sales is a real change. A broad drop in home values isn't established.

How many homes sold in Monroe County this fall?

Redfin counted 216 home sales in Monroe County in September 2026, down from 251 in September 2025. That's 35 fewer closings. Redfin labels the series "All Home Types," so it may include more than traditional resales.

As of late November, a verified, dated October series for Monroe County wasn't yet published in the sources we checked. Treat any "October Monroe County" figure you see online with caution until it's dated and labeled.

If sales fell 13.9%, why didn't prices fall 13.9%?

Because the number of sales and the price of homes measure different things. A sales count tells you how many deals closed. It can drop because fewer people listed, because higher mortgage rates priced some buyers out, or because deals took longer. None of those automatically lowers what a given house is worth.

Redfin's median sale price for the county was about $329,000, down roughly 2% from a year earlier. Redfin's page describes that figure inconsistently (as September in one place, as a three-month period in another), so it should be read as a general direction, not a precise monthly price.

What about the "Monroe County homes are up 4.46%" number?

You may see a listing site report a Monroe County median list price near $380,000, up about 4.46% year over year. That's an asking-price comparison: what sellers are requesting, not what buyers paid. It's not appreciation, and it isn't your home's value.

The gap between a roughly $380,000 asking median and a roughly $329,000 sold median also isn't a "discount." The two figures describe different homes over different periods. Homes still for sale include pricier properties that take longer to sell, which pushes the asking median up.

How do mortgage rates fit in?

The national average 30-year fixed rate was 7.28% on November 19, 2026, up from 6.26% a year earlier, according to Freddie Mac. That's a national benchmark built from loan applications, not a Monroe County rate or an individual quote.

Rates matter to sellers because they shape buyers' budgets. As an illustration: on a $328,764 home with 10% down, the monthly principal and interest on a 30-year loan is about $1,824 at 6.26% and about $2,024 at 7.28%. That's roughly $200 more a month. A buyer holding the same payment could borrow about $29,000 less. These figures exclude taxes, insurance and HOA fees.

Nationally, the National Association of Realtors reported that pending home sales in October fell 16.7% year over year. Pending sales are contracts signed, a different measure from Monroe County's closings, but they point in the same direction for activity.

Is Monroe County following the rest of Pennsylvania?

Partly. Statewide, the Pennsylvania Association of Realtors reported about 10,260 sales in September 2026, down roughly 9.5% from a year earlier, while the statewide median sale price was $315,000, up about 6%. Sales slowed in both places, and somewhat more in Monroe. Statewide prices held up better than Monroe's roughly flat reading. Statewide figures aren't a stand-in for the county.

What should Monroe County sellers do with this information?

Price your home from the homes it actually competes with, not from county headlines:

  • Use your own segment. Look at recent sales in your municipality, of your property type, in your price range.
  • Check current competition. How many similar homes are listed now, and how long have they been on the market, counting any relists?
  • Watch recent contracts. Homes going under contract show where buyers are committing today.
  • Read comps fully. Ask for each comparable home's original asking price, final asking price, and any seller credits, not just the sale price.

A slower countywide sales pace is a reason to check your competition carefully. It's not a reason, on its own, to cut your price.

What should buyers take from it?

Your negotiating room depends on the specific house. A county-wide sale-to-list ratio near 97% is an average across many sales. It isn't a rule that you can offer 3% under asking. Check the home's price history, time on market and nearby sales, and get current quotes from lenders rather than relying on the national average rate.

Not necessarily. Fewer homes are selling, and higher rates have tightened buyer budgets, but homes priced against their real competition still sell. Your timeline and your home's condition matter more than the month.

County-level data often lags statewide releases. As of late November, a verified October Monroe County series wasn't yet available in the sources checked. This article will be updated when it is.
